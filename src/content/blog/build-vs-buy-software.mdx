The build-vs-buy decision comes down to one question: **does the software fit your process, or does your process have to bend to fit the software?** Buy when your workflow matches what an off-the-shelf tool assumes — for standard needs like email, accounting, or CRM, a maintained subscription is almost always cheaper than building. Build when your process is genuinely different, when you are stitching a dozen tools together with manual copy-paste, or when the software itself is your product. The mistake is defaulting to a subscription because it feels safer, then paying for it in workarounds forever.

Neither answer is universally right. The right answer is the one that is cheapest over the life of the problem, not the one with the lowest sticker price today.

## When buying wins

- **Your process is standard.** If the tool matches how you already work, you get years of maintenance, updates, and support for a predictable monthly fee.
- **Time matters more than fit.** A subscription is live today. A custom build takes weeks. If you need to move now, buy.
- **The problem is not your differentiator.** Nobody's competitive edge is running payroll. Buy the solved problems and focus your effort where it counts.

## When building wins

- **The tool bends your workflow.** If your team is exporting to spreadsheets, re-entering data, or maintaining parallel systems just to make a tool work, you are paying for software that gets in the way.
- **You are paying for a stack of tools.** When the real cost is five subscriptions plus the human glue between them, one focused app often costs less and works better.
- **The software is the business.** If you are testing whether users will pay for a product, there is nothing to buy — that is the whole point of building.

## The no-code middle path

Between buying and writing code sits no-code tooling. For internal tools and simple workflows it is often the fastest, cheapest option — and worth trying first. It stops making sense when you hit performance limits, complex logic, or deep integrations, at which point custom code becomes the more economical choice anyway.

## How to decide without guesswork

Write down the real cost of each path: subscription over three years (including seats and add-ons) versus a custom build (up-front cost plus maintenance). Then ask whether the custom option removes a recurring cost or creates revenue. If it only adds features you will not use, buy. If it removes a cost you are paying every month, build.
