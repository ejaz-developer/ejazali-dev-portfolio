Building an MVP costs between **$15,000 and $120,000** for most founders, and the range is so wide because "MVP" means different things to different people. The honest answer is that an MVP costs whatever it takes to build the smallest thing that tests your riskiest assumption — and for some ideas that is a few weeks of focused work, for others it is three months of integrations. The number you should care about is not the total, it is whether the money buys you a real answer from real users.

The mistake that makes MVPs expensive is building the full product in the first version. An MVP exists to learn, not to impress. Every feature that does not serve the core test is cost without learning.

## What actually drives the cost

Three things move the number more than anything else:

**Scope of the core action.** If the product is one clear action — submit, book, track, order — the build is small. If it needs a dashboard, roles, and an admin panel behind that one action, the scope multiplies.

**Integrations.** Payments, mapping, scheduling, and third-party APIs each add real work: auth, error states, edge cases, and testing. Every integration is a small project of its own.

**How much you already know.** A clearly defined scope with references and examples builds faster than an idea that is still being figured out mid-build. Ambiguity is the hidden cost line.

## A realistic way to budget

Start from the riskiest assumption in your idea — the thing that would kill it if false — and build only what proves or disproves that. Then add the minimum the test needs to be credible: usually some form of sign-in, and payments if you are testing whether people will pay.

For a lean validation build, expect the lower end. For a product with accounts, real data, and integrations, expect the middle to upper range. Anything claiming a full product for a few thousand dollars is either cutting the parts that matter or learning on your dime.

## How to spend it wisely

- **Test one assumption.** A focused MVP that answers one question beats a broad one that answers none.
- **Reuse what exists.** Auth, payments, and hosting are solved problems — pay for them, don't rebuild them.
- **Design for the next decision, not the next five years.** You are buying information, not a finished system.
